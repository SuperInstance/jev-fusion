# The strongest objection to our own direction, and the best reply

Generated by running the claim through a cheap model against a shared dossier, asking
specifically for the *best version of the case against us* rather than for confirmation. That
framing is the point: a question asking "is this good" gets you a yes, and a question asking
"what is the best argument that this is worthless" gets you something usable.

## The objection, at its strongest

1. **No unique functional value.** The judge's cell selections are statistically
   indistinguishable from a blind free local-noise heuristic. It does not govern the process;
   it injects noise. And the noise costs ~100 model calls per field, which the free heuristic
   does not.

2. **Suboptimal by a wide margin.** Even granting that noise-equivalence, the judge sits 56%
   below the oracle ceiling. There is no regime in which a strategy that is both less
   effective than free and more expensive is defensible.

3. **Dimensional mismatch.** The judge emits a per-cell decision, which is functionally a
   rank-one projection of the field's continuous state. We have *ourselves* established that a
   rank-one projection cannot separate two things that differ in the directions it discards.
   So the judge cannot distinguish a genuine structural flaw from a spurious perturbation, and
   will overcorrect healthy cells while undercorrecting broken ones.

## Where it is right

**Point 1 and 2 are our own numbers.** We are not being told something uncomfortable; we are
being told the truth about a table we published. The honest response to points 1 and 2 is not
an argument. It is: *then don't build it,* and go find the task where the judge is good at
something. The roadmap item A3 — is there a task where it wins big — is the only honest
response, and it is a question rather than a defence.

**Point 3 is the most dangerous**, because it is built out of our own findings and is
internally coherent. It also happens to be the most interesting.

## The best reply to point 3 — and its limit

The objection smuggles in a conflation. It treats "deciding *whether to visit* a cell" as the
same kind of operation as "deciding *what the cell contains*."

They are not, and the difference is exactly the kind this programme keeps running into:

- **Encoding a cell's state** is a projection, and yes, a rank-one projection is lossy. That
  is the luma finding, and it is correct.
- **Selecting which cells to visit** is a *choice over a set*, and it happens in the index
  space, not the value space. The judge returns one probability per cell — that is 100 numbers
  for a 100-cell field, not one number. There is no rank-one collapse happening.

So the judge's signal is not low-dimensional. It is a *sparse* signal: one scalar per cell,
and no interaction between them. That is a different weakness from the one alleged, and it is
a real one — a sparse independent signal cannot represent "these twelve cells are wrong
*together*", which is precisely the structure a seam or a boundary produces.

**And that points at the actual experiment.** If the judge's weakness is that it cannot see
*grouped* error, then the place it should beat a free heuristic is exactly where errors come
in clusters: near a boundary, or in a contiguous misassigned region. exp4 scattered defects
uniformly, which is the one distribution least favourable to a per-cell judge and most
favourable to a free statistic.

**The falsifiable version:** re-run the selector with defects placed in a contiguous cluster
rather than scattered, and measure whether the judge overtakes the free heuristic. If it
does not, the objection wins completely and Lane A closes. If it does, the judge is good at
exactly one thing and we should build only that.
